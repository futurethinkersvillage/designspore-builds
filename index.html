I The inversion

The biggest money managers own a sliver of everything. We’d rather own all of something small, and do it properly.

Somewhere along the way, investing came to mean holding a thin slice of the whole economy and caring about none of it. Rents go up, the town empties out, the dividend arrives on schedule.

White Rock runs the arithmetic the other way round. We take large, long positions in a handful of rural places and judge them by what they produce: food on the table, power in the line, and families who decide to stay another winter. The returns are real — they just come from the harvest instead of the rent roll.

II Three kinds of sovereignty

A place that can get itself through a bad year.

That’s the whole test. When the highway closes, the grid goes down or the price of everything doubles, can the valley still eat, keep warm and keep working? We fund the things that make the answer yes.

  1. Food the pantry

    Fields, greenhouses, cold storage and a kitchen that buys from them. We fund the unglamorous infrastructure — the cold room, the processing kitchen, the irrigation line — that turns a short northern season into a year of meals.

  2. Energy the hearth

    Sun on the roofs, water in the creeks, wood in the stove and batteries to carry it all through the night. Every holding gets a power plan that keeps the lights on when the line comes down in January.

  3. Gathering the hall

    Housing, workshops, kitchens and halls that let people earn a living where they live. Fibre internet and a CNC machine belong in the same plan as the root cellar — trades, remote work, families and makers under one roofline.

A valley that can feed itself, power itself and keep its young people is worth more than any index can say. We intend to put a number on it anyway — in harvests, kilowatt-hours and winters stayed.

III Holdings

Two properties in one valley, to start.

We start where we live: the Clearwater valley in British Columbia, Canada, at the doorstep of Wells Gray Provincial Park.

Two neighbouring properties, two different starting points. One already houses and entertains thousands of guests a season on 400 acres of open ground. The other already runs a restaurant, a hotel and a cattle ranch. Put together, they have most of what a valley needs to feed, power and house itself — they just haven’t had capital that was willing to sit still long enough to finish the job.

IV The model

Slow money, laid down in three layers.

  1. The harvest returns

    Investors are paid from what the place produces — meals served, kilowatt-hours sold, nights stayed — not from rent hikes or resale. Upside is shared with the people who live and work there.

  2. The topsoil operations

    Farms, kitchens, lodging and power, run by local operators who own a real share of what they run. We back operators; we don’t replace them with managers from head office.

  3. The bedrock the ground

    Land goes into a long-term holding and stays there. It isn’t flipped, leveraged to the hilt or sold out from under the people building on it. Everything else stands on this.

What we look for
  1. Ground that can grow food and make power
  2. An operator who already lives on it
  3. A nearby town that wants it to work
  4. Room to house people, and the services to do it
  5. A reason for visitors to come — hospitality carries the early years
  6. A founder willing to measure success in decades

Terms are illustrative. White Rock Equity is a concept in development; nothing on this site is an offer to sell or a solicitation to buy any security.

V Principles

Nine lines, cut in stone so we can’t quietly edit them later.

  1. IThe ground is never flipped.

    We hold land the way a family farm does: to hand it on.

  2. IIThe pantry before the clubhouse.

    Food and power get funded before anything decorative.

  3. IIIThe lights stay on in January.

    Every holding is designed around its worst week, not its best.

  4. IVOperators own part of what they run.

    The people who do the work share in what it earns.

  5. VProfit is spent in the valley first.

    Local trades, local suppliers, local wages, then dividends.

  6. VINo home is bought to be rented back to its town.

    We build housing. We don’t corner it.

  7. VIIAll of a few places, not a sliver of every place.

    Concentration is how you end up caring.

  8. VIIITechnology is a tool for the place.

    Fibre, solar, software and robots — all welcome, all in service of living well here.

  9. IXMeasure in winters stayed.

    The best sign a place is working: people choose to stay through the dark months.
